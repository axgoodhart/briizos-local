# Directions for Reproducing the Essay Style

*Derived from the four essays in this folder: "The Conflict of Contrasts" (Bartók, Contrasts), "Playing Beethoven through Beethoven" (Bagatelle Op. 126 No. 2), "Death & Transfiguration: a reimagining of Debussy" (Des pas sur la neige), and "Ruhe meine Seele: Thoughts on the Opportunity Within Contradiction" (Strauss, Vier Lieder).*

---

## 1. What the Essay Is For

An essay in this style is an interpretive analysis written for the performer. It does analysis, but analysis is never where it stops. Every theoretical observation (a harmonic table, a count of measures, a comparison of autographs) is gathered so that the essay can ask what a musician on stage should do with it, and how an audience will receive the result. The writer stands between the theorist's desk and the performer's bench and keeps facing the bench.

So the essay should always know who its reader is. That reader is a thinking musician, someone who can follow a Roman-numeral analysis or a tonnetz but who has to play the piece next week. Phrases like "the performer," "the analytic performer," "musicians and audiences," and "the player" come up throughout, and nearly every section closes by turning toward them. A paragraph that ends on a theoretical finding without reaching the performer feels unfinished in this voice.

The essays also carry an ethic beneath the practical advice. Interpretation is legitimate, even necessary, invention. Performers are "acting, always." History is something to imagine as much as to verify. Authority, whether it belongs to the HIP purist, the conductor who claims "the true movement," or the analyst who wants a single correct reading, gets treated with polite skepticism. The essay hands the reader permission as well as information.

## 2. The Governing Idea: One Tension, Held Open

Each essay is built around a single productive contradiction, named early and carried to the end:

- Bartók: the composer asks for *contrast*, yet the material is so dense that contrast collapses into smoothness (the "nitrogen bubbles").
- Strauss: a song called "Rest, my soul!" that contains almost no rest, with an exclamation point undoing its own request.
- Beethoven: a late piece written in the manner of his youth, so the performer has to hold a dying present and a vigorous past at once.
- Debussy: a prelude about footprints, which are traces of bodies that are no longer there, so its imagery is small and its gravity is large.

To reproduce the style, find the paradox first. Look for a place where the title argues with the music, the notation argues with the sound, the period argues with the date, or the composer's stated intention argues with the listener's experience. The essay doesn't settle the contradiction by choosing a side. It treats the contradiction as the opportunity, the thing the performer can dramatize. The Strauss subtitle states the method outright: "the Opportunity Within Contradiction."

The conclusion should restate the tension as a balanced paradox rather than dissolve it. The model is the Bartók ending: "its density creates space, its ambiguity invites meaning."

## 3. Titles and Headings

**Titles** are layered. They usually have a main title that turns on wordplay, inversion, or borrowed grandeur, plus a lowercase or parenthetical subtitle that says plainly what the essay is:

- "The Conflict of Contrasts / an interpretive analysis with Bartók"
- "an unconventional crossing of conventions: Playing Beethoven through Beethoven"
- "Death & Transfiguration (a reimagining of Debussy) (in two parts)": a Strauss title borrowed for a Debussy essay, which is a cross-reference joke in itself
- "Ruhe meine Seele / Thoughts on the Opportunity Within Contradiction"

Favor reflexive or chiastic constructions (X through X, the conflict of contrasts, a crossing of conventions), borrowed titles, and words that point at the essay's central paradox.

**Section headings** are short and a little literary. Three patterns recur:

1. An indefinite article plus an abstract noun, building a small arc: "A Conflict," "A Story," "A Realization," then "Conclusion."
2. Numbered stages that trace the argument's journey: "1. Beethoven and his Past / 2. Dated Considerations / 3. Through Beethoven, Self." Note the pun in "Dated" and the inversion in the last heading, which echoes the title.
3. Lowercase Roman numerals with a colon-split pair that plays on the essay's central image: "ii. in footprints : a prelude," "iii. many prints : many paths."

The headings should sound like chapter titles in a slim book of criticism, not like labels in a report. "Introduction" and "Conclusion" are allowed as plain bookends.

## 4. Openings

There are four tested ways in. Pick one.

- **The everyday analogy.** Start far from music with a sensory image the reader already knows, and only then bring it to the score. Example: "Think of the 'mouth feel,' if you've partaken, of a Guinness Draught stout versus that of a hard seltzer." The analogy should carry a counterintuitive point (smaller bubbles make a smoother texture) that maps exactly onto the musical problem, and it should come back later as shorthand ("a piece composed of nitrogen bubbles").
- **The epigraph and the scholarly frame.** Open with a quotation from the composer, then describe how scholarship usually treats this music and position the essay against that habit. Example: Debussy on "dismantling them like curious watches," followed by the observation that analysts approach late Debussy "with humility."
- **The direct analytic claim.** Begin mid-thought with evidence: "In the attached harmonic analysis excerpt, we find that Beethoven is working in familiar milieu…" Data follows quickly, usually a striking number.
- **Reading the title.** Take the work's own name at its word and find the crack in it: "Rest, My Soul, rendered less than restful with the added exclamation point!"

Whichever opening you use, the essay's paradox should appear within the first page, and there should be a sentence that declares the plan in the first person ("Here, in its first movement, I will examine…").

## 5. The Shape of the Argument

The typical movement runs like this:

1. **Observation.** Something concrete in the score, often quantified.
2. **Widening.** A comparison to other works by the same composer, by predecessors, or across the repertoire, sometimes with a list or table.
3. **Question.** What does this mean? Often several questions in a row.
4. **Interpretive frame.** Narrative, drama, empathy, historical imagination, or a borrowed analytical lens such as Renaissance polyphony, Baroque practice, or neo-Riemannian space.
5. **Performer's application.** Specific, technical suggestions: an accent-plus-tenuto on a common-tone bass E, non-legato within each voice, no rubato in the sixteenth-note passages and rubato in the dyads, energy placed at the start of a phrase rather than its apex, dynamics read per voice.
6. **Return to the paradox**, now enriched.

Inside that arc, the essay deliberately narrows and widens its lens. It names its own scope limits, often with a twist that turns the limit into a virtue:

- "The historicity of an emotional-temporal perspective lies beyond the intentions of this analysis; this, itself, though, is freeing."
- "Deserving more consideration (than can be allowed here)…"
- "We will continue to focus solely on the first phrase as an invitation…"

It also circles back on itself. The Beethoven essay's third part opens by nearly repeating a sentence from the second ("Returning to where we began…"), and then restates its own method in the past tense ("Instead, I suggested…"). Strategic self-quotation like this, which tells the reader where they are, is part of the style. That fits, because the essays argue that audiences need to know where they are in a piece.

## 6. Voice and Stance

**Person.** The writer moves freely among "I," "we," and a third-person self-reference ("the writer here answers affirmatively," "the author sees continual potential," "the author hopes"). "We" is used for the community of musicians: "As performers, we are acting." "I" appears for proposals and invitations: "I propose," "I encourage one to consider," "I invite musicians and audiences," "I'd posit, though." Use the third-person "author" sparingly, at moments of formal gravity or self-aware modesty.

**Confidence.** The voice holds firm positions but presents them as proposals. Hedges like "Perhaps," "may," "it can be noted," "it could be," and "seems" are frequent, yet the paragraph's conclusion is usually unhedged. The rhythm is a tentative approach followed by a decisive landing. Then, at the right moment, the writer declines to dictate: "I think, though, that is no place of mine to dictate how one should formulate this empathy."

**Polemic, lightly worn.** Opponents are named as attitudes rather than people: the strict historicist, the dogmatic performer, the analyst who forces frameworks. Rebuttal arrives through irony rather than attack:

- "After all, a Historically Informed Performance wouldn't deign to consider the extramusical emotions of its composer… Perish the thought!"
- "It is a fiction that 'Argerich plays the music, Lang Lang displays the music.'"

**Humor.** Humor is essential and usually comes in one of these forms:

- *Anthropomorphic comedy.* Instruments become characters with grievances: "the greedy clarinet moved right from its saccharine melody to ostentatious folk mode scaling and trills before having the gall to go off and gallivant about with piano!"
- *Imagined scenes with dialogue.* A harried choral director arranging the prelude: "basses: this isn't really so difficult, just a perfect fifth with delusions of grandeur… yes, you can sing that pickup D down the octave…"
- *Reductio.* Pushing a bad method to absurdity: "it isn't long before we are extrapolating fugues out of Die schöne Müllerin and atonality from Johannespassion."
- *Insider asides.* "ask any jazzer," "a dash of whimsy," "(what was likely never on Beethoven's music stand)."

The jokes always make the argument. Each one is a compressed version of the interpretive point, never decoration. Use about one or two per essay at full strength, with wry asides scattered elsewhere.

**Humanism.** At least one passage should step back and speak about people: composers as fallible humans, audiences who need meaning, performers as actors. These passages can lean on a long catalog: "Beethoven's fallibility, weakness, alcoholism, passion, clumsiness, anger, joy, embarrassment, non-pretension, disability, expectation, rigor, pride, fondnesses, even fears…"

## 7. Sentence-Level Habits

**Long, hinged sentences.** The default sentence is long and built from clauses joined by colons, semicolons, and dashes. The colon does the heaviest lifting, setting up a claim and then delivering it: "It is nearly paradoxical: in our ignorant physiological self awareness, we may easily assume…"

**Then a short one.** Long sentences are regularly punctured by a fragment or a very short sentence:

- "Unconventional, pseudohistorical? Yes."
- "No: the greedy clarinet…"
- "…seven indications."
- "But that is not this prelude."

**Fronted and inverted constructions.** Sentences often begin with the predicate or an evaluative adjective:

- "Important to remember is that Debussy's training…"
- "Preferable, too, is whether…"
- "Doubtless, though, that some media are more reliant on time."
- "Such is the same approach Beethoven is taking in this Bagatelle."

**Rhetorical questions, in volleys.** Questions arrive in clusters of three or four, often escalating or narrowing:

- "But of what character is this state? Disturbance? Trepidation? Anxiety?"
- "Why does the air not move? Why is the shade characterized as something without light, some thing that conceals? And why is the glimmer of sun's warmth a thief?"

There is also the long cumulative question built by anaphora, as in the Beethoven "whose… whose… whose…" sentence, which piles up historical examples until the question answers itself.

**Parentheses everywhere.** Parentheses hold qualifications, concessions, and side-glances: "(an admittedly large portion of the total)," "(that is, a bit blunted to nuance…)," "(less likely originally, due to France's late adoption)." A balanced pair of parenthetical triplets is a signature move: "Beethoven's (healthier, happier, Mozartian) past and his (beleaguered, beloved, dying) present."

**Slash-stacked alternatives.** When one word won't do, offer two or three joined by slashes: "the enjoyment of / engagement with / participation in music," "observed/understood/absorbed," "jealousy/contempt," "idea/answer (or call/response)," "diminished/half-diminished/augmented-sixth." This reads as precision through plurality, a refusal to pick one word too early.

**Triads and catalogs.** Adjectives and nouns come in threes or fours: "profundity, depth, lushness, and menace"; "fragile, disembodied, yet deeply personal relics"; "deaf, great, misunderstood, and his body failing"; "fiery, rigid, storming the Viennese scene."

**Dashes.** Dashes are frequent, used for interruption, apposition, and pivot. Across the essays they appear as spaced en dashes, as doubled dashes ("––"), and as em dashes. Pick one form per essay and use it generously, often several times a paragraph.

**The single exclamation.** Exclamation points are rare and always ironic or theatrical ("Perish the thought!", the clarinet's "gall"). Use one or two per essay at most.

## 8. Diction

The vocabulary mixes elevated, Latinate words with the occasional colloquial drop, and the contrast is deliberate.

- **Elevated:** milieu, cohabitate, edifying, veritably, kaleidoscopic, paucity, vexations, consolidation, amelioration, whence, deign, eschew, travails (used transitively), pollenated, elucidate, illusive, profundity.
- **Coined or compounded technical terms:** "always-implied-never-played tonic," "emotional-temporal perspective," "psychosensory experience," "étudinal," "melodicization," "pseudohistorical," "functionally agnostic tonnetz," "structural cleavages," "collaborating in opposition." Coin freely when an existing term is too blunt, and hyphenate long compounds into a single adjective.
- **Foreign phrases**, used unitalicized and unglossed: idée fixe, sans l'histoire, in absentia, oeuvre, concerto grosso, praeludium, obbligato. Quote the composer's own markings in the original language and translate them in parentheses.
- **Colloquial drops:** "a bit," "ask any jazzer," "Erm," "more than a little help," "Of course: pitch class sets, generative theory, etc.!" These keep the register from becoming stiff, so place them strategically around grandiosity, but not in the same reliable way.

Characteristic verbs include *illuminate, elucidate, render, embody, animate, foreground, invoke, imbue, reconcile, dramatize, subvert*. Characteristic nouns include *narrative, through-line, affect, drama, duality, dichotomy, ambiguity, contradiction, empathy, imagination, lens, framework, modality*.

## 9. Evidence and Apparatus

The essays support their claims with a lot of concrete material, set into the prose.

- **Numbers that surprise.** "comprising 89 measures of 4/4, the piece's tonic of C minor is dispersed into what amounts to only 4 measures, plus one and a half beats." "An excerpt… of just thirty-two measures gives… seven indications." Count something in the score that the reader hasn't counted.
- **Enumerations with a setup line.** "In four measures, we have:" followed by a numbered list, or "For clarity, an enumeration:" followed by dashed items. A list can pivot mid-stream with a lone connective ("but,") to place two sets against each other.
- **Tables and parallel columns.** Harmonic analysis of the same progression read in two keys side by side. Original-language text beside its translation. A full catalog of the composer's works, laid out to make a point about what is missing from it.
- **Score excerpts and visual comparisons.** Autograph comparisons, open-score reductions, "melodic passages only" versus "score in full," tonnetz plots comparing several song cycles, short excerpts from other works placed next to the subject piece. Each image gets a caption naming composer, work, and movement ("C. Debussy / String Quartet, III.").
- **Wide comparative reach.** Pull in predecessors (Bach, Daquin, Gabrieli), contemporaries and successors (Mahler, Barber, Shostakovich, Messiaen), the composer's own other works (the Pathétique, Op. 57, Op. 110), and even the author's own compositions. Name famous performers when making a point about performance culture.
- **Methodological pluralism with a conscience.** Combine functional harmony, Schenkerian reduction, neo-Riemannian transformation, pitch-class sets, and Renaissance contrapuntal thinking, but state a principle that disciplines the mixing (fidelity "to coherence and continuity," frameworks that should "apply to music other than the piece at hand"). The essay earns its freedom by naming its rules.

## 10. Interpretive Frames to Reach For

The essays return to a small family of frames. A new essay should use one as its main lens:

- **Narrative and drama.** Music unfolds in time, audiences need to know "where they are," and performers need a story. This can be the composer's, the performer's, or an imagined one.
- **Anthropomorphized ensemble.** Instruments as characters in rivalry, dialogue, or submission. The fiction is defended as a practical tool, because it settles a hundred small decisions at once.
- **Historical imagination.** History treated "in the realm of imagination" rather than accuracy, with empathy for the composer as a person.
- **Borrowed period practice.** Reading a piece through an earlier idiom, such as Baroque articulation for Beethoven or Renaissance polyphony for Debussy, to reveal the work's linear or rhetorical structure.
- **Audience psychology.** What the listener can actually perceive, given modern ears "a bit blunted to nuance" by a "broader and often decontextualized palette."
- **Internalized contradiction.** Having each performer embody the work's contradiction within their own part, not only between parts (tension at the start of an ascent rather than its apex, restraint where the notation invites force).

## 11. Endings

The conclusion gathers the paradox, the frame, and the performer into one or two elevated paragraphs. It typically:

- names the frame as a proposal ("I propose that drama… as an interpretive lens…"),
- claims for it a unifying power ("a through-line," "a unifying force," "cohesion"),
- turns the paradox into an aphorism,
- and echoes the title, either by quoting it ("a 'conflict of contrasts'") or by mirroring its structure ("through Beethoven's life as they've tried and come to know it, or, in other words, through themselves").

For a multi-part essay, end the part on a forward-looking question and a final sentence that folds the title into the image: "There, we will find a bird's eye view of the footprints themselves, and, the author hopes, be transfigured by the death it reveals."

The register lifts at the end, with words like *resonant, journey, humanity, transcend, rewarding*. The lift is earned because the preceding pages were so concrete.

## 12. Page and Presentation

The essays are typeset documents, and the look is part of the voice:

- A title page or title block with the author's full name set apart, sometimes with stylized or mixed-case lettering.
- Section headings centered, often with a decorative drop cap opening the first paragraph of each section.
- A running footer in the form "Goodhart: [Short Title]  Page X of Y."
- Epigraphs set off and attributed with a dash and the composer's name in capitals.
- Generous whitespace between paragraphs, and figures placed inline next to the sentence that discusses them.

## 13. A Brief Checklist

Before calling a draft finished in this style, check that:

- One paradox is named on the first page and is still alive in the last paragraph.
- The title has a turn in it, and the headings form a small arc.
- At least one surprising number or count from the score appears early.
- There is at least one enumeration, one table or side-by-side comparison, and one cross-repertoire excerpt.
- Every analytic section arrives at something a performer can physically do.
- The essay names the limits of its own scope and makes that limit a virtue.
- There is a cluster of rhetorical questions, a short fragment after a long sentence, and an inverted "Important to remember is…" construction.
- One extended comic set piece (anthropomorphism, imagined dialogue, or reductio) makes the argument by joking.
- Parentheses, slashes, and dashes are doing real work.
- The ending proposes, unifies, turns the paradox into an aphorism, and echoes the title.
